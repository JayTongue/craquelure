At my workplace, we have a staff development fund which is a pot of money people can apply for in order to engage in work or non-work related activates. Obviously it's not going to be a lot, but some other example I've heard other employees have applied for money to take ceramics classes, get SCUBA certifications, or attend non-work related conferences.

I've been interested in learning more about data processing and analytics for a while. I think knowledge about the more mathematical side of dealing with data has been a bit of a blind spot for me. Many places recommended the Google Data Analytics course on Coursera, so I looked into that. The course listing says that it takes most learners 3-6 months to complete, so I figured I'd apply to our staff development fund for 6 months of membership. At $59/month, my application was granted for anywhere around $300 in total.

When I went to go register for the service, however, I found out that there's no way to purchase a number of months all at once. You have to pay for each upcoming month as the current months expires. This meant that I would have to fill out the expense for every single month. I also found out that Coursera was running a promo for their annual account membership for $279. I figured no one would mind since I was costing the fund less and wouldn't have to bother them with my monthly expense forms, and registered for that instead.

Let the Year of Coursera Commence.

These are the courses I took (or tried) and how I would grade them.

1. Google: Google Data Analytics (DNF)

This is the course that I originally applied for funding to take. I really wanted to like this course. I got about a module in, when I realized that this would be far too simple for me. There were several things that made me feel like this would just mostly waste my time. The first module was very broad. Like waxing philosophical on "what is data" broad. The material in that first module was also focused on stuff that they, for lack of a better term, just sort of made up. For instance, Google came up with this 4-step approach to Data Analytics called PACE (Plan, Analyze, Construct, Execute). So many assessments in that first module would be like "Here's a situation. what step of PACE is this?" PACE really isn't bad, but I don't care about it that much since it's something that you just made up.

The nail in the coffin was when the course started explaining different tools data professionals often use- Spreadsheets (that's Google Sheets, mind you, NOT MS Excel), R, and Python. Then they said that this class would focus on spreadsheets and R, since Python was too "advanced" for the scope of the class. Nah fam. Nothing against R or spreadsheets, but I think I can handle Python.

2. Google: Google Advanced Data Analytics (B+)

This is the "Advanced" version of the normal Google Data Analytics course, where you're trusted with the full, untempered power of Guido Van Rossum's unholy creation. I was confirmed in my choice to jump to this when I took the initial "readiness" assessment and scored a 94% without taking the entry level course.

I think this course is half good. There are 8 total sub-courses, and I would say that 4 of them are useful, of which 3 were informative to me. One of them was an "intro to Python" module, which if I were new to Python, probably would have been really helpful. I more or less clicked through it though.

Within each of these sub-courses, there are 4-7 modules. Each of these modules is made up of several videos, readings, and assessments. This may seem like a lot, but each module has intro and conclusion videos that have no hard information, as well as sometimes having a video on someone telling their story about how they ended doing data at Google.

The meat of the substance is in the videos and readings. The videos are of a rotating cast of hosts explaining information to you. These are very good. Every aspect of the production is polished, from staging and lighting to scripting, it's clear that some very smart people were paid fairly to care a lot about this. The hosts are pretty good at explaining ideas that otherwise could much more difficult to approach due to their complexity. The readings are mostly a recap of the content in the video, but not always. Often times, the readings will cover more information, which then shows up in the assessments.

The three sub-courses that I found to be very informative were the chapters on 4. Statistics, 5. Regression Analysis, and 6. Machine Learning. While I was going through these, I felt like I was actually enrolled in a class. I was taking notes and going over them to make sure that I understood the material.

Overall, I give this course a B+. Although there really is great information and instructional material in here, the course overall feels bloated. How many conclusion videos do you need that all start with some variation of "Wow, look at how far you've come in your Data Analytics journey!" or piddly little quizzes to check for understanding? However, I'd still recommend this course to someone who wants to learn these topics. Note, however, that you do need some mathematical and computational background.

3. Packt: Intro to IoT Pentesting (B-)

I wanted to take this course because I'd been playing around with hardware security testing for a bit. I've pulled open some home networking devices, gotten a shell, pulled firmware, and analyzed it. What I wanted to learn more about was firmware analysis, attack surface analysis, and other methods of pentesting.

This course's strength is its breadth. It covers everything from different tools you can use to perform pentests to how to scan for open ports, to how to how to see whether embedded devices can be matched to known exploits. The weakness of this course, however, is that it doens't go into depth in any of these. It will show you how to do something like how to use Hydra or Shodan to exploit a device, but not what to do if your target device isn't specifically that one. In short, things come across as more of a demonstration an a lesson.

For me, I also found that this course was review. I already had a lot of the cheaper equipment, knew how to connect to UART shells, identify JTAG pins, use Binwalk, and several other skills. The videos on these didn't really teach me much more than I already knew. For someone else though, these are likely very informative videos.

I will say though that each video feels very padded out with chatter between the hosts. It's fine, but it's a presentation style that seems geared more towards entertainment than pedagogy, but for a course this short, it's not a problem at all.

4. Packt: Mastering Hardware Protocols with ESP32 and Arduinos (A+)

This course is very informative and straight forward. It goes through the way that common microcontroller protocols (UART, OneWire, SPI, I2C, etc) are used, and breaks them down into the actual binary signals that these procols use. The instructor also shows you how to write a lot of these libraries yourself, in case you don't want to use a pre-made library. It also goes over how to use a logic analyzer with Sigrok's Pulseview, which is something I've been wanting to jump into for some upcoming projects.

Overall great course. Nothing I really want to critique, and just good content with good presentation.

5. University of Rochester: The Blues: Understanding and Performing an American Art Form (A-)

This was the first Non-STEM class that I took and I wanted to see what that experience would be like. The first thing that threw me for a loop was the fact that there's some Polish guy telling me about the uniquely American development of Blues and Jazz. But boy does he do a good job.

Music Ed is difficult and made more difficult over a distance. So much of what it means to understand something in music comes down to listening to something and "getting" it. This is what it means to understand something like tension-release or ii-V-I. You can analyze the music, but to you have to listen to it to "get" it. The instructor for this course does a really good job with this. He explains things, then demonstrates them in a way that allows the student to "get" it.

In the end, I actually like the fact that Jazz was a much more fluent language to the instructor than English. Where an English-native instructor might lean on descriptive like "jazzy" or "bluesy", he another set of vocabulary, like explaining how a melodic line is "effective" or a chord's "sonority". This is a refreshing change from the set of descriptors I'm used to hearing with jazz.

Am I better at playing the Blues after this? I feel like that's where the course sort of fails. Despite there being a lot of explanation and information about Blues, there's not enough in terms of practical exercises.

6. University of Colorado Boulder: Introduction to Embedded Systems Software and Development Environments (DNF)

I really wanted to like this course. I've been tinkering with microcontrollers for a while, and have been fascinated by lower-level languages, so this course on writing bare metal C for microcontrollers seemed like the perfect fit. The instructor is genuinely knowledgeable and decent at explaining challenging concepts.

So why the DNF? Well, one of the materials required for the course is a Texas Instruments MSP432 Development Kit. Not a big deal since it's a 10$ microcontroller, or at least it could be if it wasn't discontinued in 2021. As such, I got through about 2/3 of the course when I ran into assignments that I needed to submit that I simply could not do because I didn't have the equipment. This course needs a serious refresh to remedy this, preferably switching over to an ESP32, RP2040, Arduino or another controller that people can actually access.

7. Imperial College: Mathematics for Machine Learning (A)

I feel like this course was the high water mark when it comes to what a Coursera course can be. Full disclosure, I hadn't really done any math since High School when I tapped out after Calc AB. This course sort of perfectly picks up where that left off, but applied what I was learning to computing.

This course has three sections: Linear Algebra, Multivariate Calculus, and Principal component analysis (PCA). Overall, all these modules consist of subtopics with video lectures and assignments along the way. There are two kinds of assignments: Labs and quizzes. Labs are Jupyter notebooks with graded function definitions that you complete. The quizzes were challenging. By analogy, if a lecture covers A, B, and C, the quizzes will make you demonstrate that you can do AB, BC, and ABC. These are often an excellent check for understanding. Conversely, Labs feel genuinely interactive. Since you don't have to do math (python will do that for you), you just have to define your functions, and the notebooks offer genuine direction and assistance in doing so.

However, I will say that while Linear Algebra and Multivariate Calc were stellar, there is a quality shift when you get to PCA. Firstly, it's a different instructor who uses different shorthand than the other two did. Something like using "X^TX" instead of "X@X" for dot product can get really confusing when it all stacks up and comes at you fast and heavy. I think that some of this is because the first two instructors teach material science, and the last instructor teaches machine learning. I know that different disciplines often use different conventions when representing the same idea, and that might be what's at play here.

The last section on PCA is a trial by fire. The instructor has no mercy, and expects you to already be fluent enough with the math to follow along. His lectures are fast and awkward, and often don't even cover the entire subject, leaning on additionally linked readings. This is likely due to the fact that PCA is just such a large topic. There's also an issue with the autograder, where your assignment will show up as 0 points unless you download the notebook it and upload it separately. Thank you peer forum for figuring that one out.

I would have given this course an A+ but for the PCA section.

8. Skillshare: Learn to Draw (B+)

This class was interesting. It's taught by Brent Eviston, and when I went to his website, it looked like the same course was being offered for 100$, so doing it through Coursera is pretty good deal. It's hard for me to be objective about the material of this course because I took a drawing class in college. A lot of the explanation and instruction in this course just reminded me of something I had already learned. The videos are very professional and well done, and his presentation style is very simple and clear. It's just hard for me to take that and say whether a true beginner would have similarly benefited from the course.

One thing I really love about this class is that he pulls no punches about the fact that only practice will make you better. In a world where hobbies are increasingly dominated by spending money, it's very refreshing to hear that pretty much any paper will do and any pencil will do when you're first starting out.

Did I end up drawing more? Actually, yes. I feel like having those ideas at the front of my brain removes a bit of friction to picking up a pencil and doing a bit of a drawing.

UPDATE: right before I pushed this post, I found out that Brent Eviston launched an app that uses AI to give you feedback on your art. I generally don't want to be automatically dismissive, but there are few things I want AI to do less than to judge art.

9. The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology: Heath Concepts in Chinese Medicine (DNF)

I really wanted to like this course. I minored in Asian Studies in undergrad, and I remember really being really fascinated by the medicinal topics of Daoism when I took a class on that. I wanted this course to deepen my understanding of those medicinal and philosophical traditions. The problem is that these lectures are so boring and rote. The material and their presentation comes off more like an advertisement or infomercial than either an academic course. It presents the conceptual framework underlying Chinese medicinal practices, but the material is not really engaging or interesting at all.

10. Wesleyan University: The Modern and the Postmodern (Parts 1 and 2) (A-)

This is a survey course of European philosophical thought from the enlightenment to the modern day. The presenter, Michael Roth, is a genuinely engaging and clear presenter. He shares information and ideas in a way that is approachable and digestible, while not watering down the depth and complexity of the thinkers he presents.

There are essays. A total of 8 essays, with an option 9th one. I was not prepared to have to do work for this course. You also have to read books. But it wasn't all that bad. I got to do essay writing, which I haven't done since undergrad, covering thinkers like Kant, Rousseau, Flaubert, Woolf, Wittgenstein, Butler, and West. It was pretty fun to be able to sit down and bang out a focused few paragraphs about a singular argument. Coursera does use AI to grade the essays, which is sort of a bummer, but it's fine? I'm not looking for like real critical academic feedback, so I know that if I have a clear organization, write at least at a collegiate level, and include quotes, then it'll give me a pass and I'll move on to the next module.

But in addition to your own essays, you also have to grade other people's and boy is that a crapchute. Sometimes these are low-effort LLM slop. Sometimes the person wrote just three sentences. Sometimes it's not even in English. Coursera really fails at making peer-grading of any value.

This course gets an A- because the material and presentation is high-quality and very enjoyable, but there are flaws.

11. MoMA: Seeing through Photographs (B)

This was a quick survey course that covered the history of photography. It presents quick intros to photographers from some of the beginning of the technology through various steps of democratization and digitization. As an amateur photographer, I was already familiar with a number of photographers, but this course showed me how narrow my knowledge and familiarity actually was. It was great to break out of my narrow lane and see more of the world of photographic work.

This course gets a B because although the readings and videos are high-quality, structurally it suffers from valuing breath over depth. There are a lot of ideas that are merely introduced, but instead of giving it enough discussion, it links to an external resource, which was invariably a paid book published by MoMA. If this were a University course that had the same breath, I think it would be great. Students could use the professor as a resource to have deeper discussions and discover more resources about the ideas or photographers that excite them. But absent this, the format falls flat.

Additionally, the course has some Coursera-isms. For instance, the readings have an automated AI voice that you can use to read it to you. There was a photograph title/caption that was in Spanish, and the AI decided to read the rest of the reading in Spanish after that. Also, there were some activities you have to complete which are to do a chat conversation with an AI, and I hated those.

12. Edureka!: Shell Scripting in Linux (D)

I made a comment to some friends online a little while ago that learning bash would probably be more useful for work than learning C. Well, with 7 days left in my subscription, I thought I'd see what Coursera actually offers for bash. After looking at a couple courses, this one seemed both short enough and in-depth enough to be useful.

I really liked that it doesn't try to teach you programming principles, and is very language focused, but could be a reason a beginner would struggle with it. the presenter does an okay job, but his screen recordings have minuscule, low-contrast text and he has a heavy accent, so it can be a bit a little challenging to follow. The real problem, however, is that despite the fact that it claims to teach "advanced" concepts, these really weren't advanced. It touched on a lot of concepts and commands that may be useful for a sysadmin, but not for using it as an everyday turing-complete scripting language. This sort of makes sense since this module was part of their Linux sysadmin specialization, but still. Don't call things "Advanced" then barely touch on core concepts like file IO.

The assignments though are complete trash. A lot of them ask about information that the videos didn't cover, and they're just meaningless multiple choice checks. This course would be so much stronger with an actual interactive coding module like the Imperial Math course had. Instead, it has fake "chats" that you have to do with an AI. Ah yes. Let me type out an entire bash script in your crappy chat editor with no indentation or formatting.

By far the worst course I completed.

---

Overall:

So although I applied to take one course, and I ended up getting 12. From their variety, you can see my wide gambit of interests, and it was very fun to be able to have an additional resource to dive into all of those.

Do I think Coursera is worth it? Possibly. There are some aspects where I think Coursera genuinely excels. Across the board, the quality of the material on Coursera is very high. These are more like online courses than something you'd find for free somewhere like Youtube, since the courses are made by institutions that put actual resources into it. For the most part, these have polished material, well produced audio and video, etc. The platform itself is also pretty good to use, with minimal bugs.

On the negative side, if you have something that has to mechanistically graded, e.g. submitting code or answering questions, then Coursera is great. However, if you have something like an essay, then it's either an AI doing it or you're waiting forever for peer grading, if it ever happens. Also negative is the fact that some courses have a lot of "filler". Like little "memory check" assignments, or other things that are probably included to meet some arbitrary goal for time spent in the course or number of activities.

I think that picking a course to take is an underrated skill. Obviously if you need a particular certificate from a company like Google or CompTIA, then you're going to take that course. But if you're just trying to learn a skill, especially a technical skill, there are probably multiple course offerings with similar titles. By spending a little time with the course description, you can get a feeling for

1. Topics covered

2. Depth it goes into

3. Length of the course

4. Number of assignments

Then when you start taking a course, you'll find out:

5. Quality of the material

6. Pleasant/annoying level of the presenter(s)

7. Usefulness of assignments

So would I buy it myself? Honestly, probably not, unless I needed the certificate for an employer or something. Although there is very high quality material on the platform, all of this information is available elsewhere, maybe in a better format, lower cost, or both. This is especially true with technical/programming courses. There have been times when a course looks interesting, but I see that it's based on a book the professor published, and I skipped the course in favor of reading the book.

In the end, I feel like Coursera is a good platform and if you have a learning style that matches up to this sort of modular structure, then it may be the best one for you. For me, however, it worked fine but did not particularly resonate with me.
