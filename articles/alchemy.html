1. Hard and Soft

"It's not going to work" "Oh yeah? Why is that, Granger?" "You see this? It's an age line. Dumbledore drew it himself." "So?" "So, a genius like Dumbledore couldn't possibly be fooled by dodges pathetically dim-witted as an aging potion."

In Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire, Fred and George try to use an aging potion to put their names into the goblet. But Hermione's retort that "Dumbledore drew it himself" always felt odd to me. My assumption is that the age line is a type of magic that works consistently in a specific way. Either it can be bypassed by an aging potion or not, regardless of who drew it. But what Hermione indicates is that the functionality of the age line is not consistent at all. If the same magic is used by a more powerful wizard, it will have different behavior than if it were used by someone with weaker magic.

Later in the film, Dumbledore, Mad-Eye Moody, Madame Maxime, and Igor Karkaroff confront Harry about how his name was selected by the goblet.

"HARRY did you put your name in the Goblet of Fire?" "No sir." "Ask one of the older students to do it for you?" "No sir." "You absolutely sure?" "Yes sir." "But of course he is lying!" "The hell he is. The goblet of fire is an exceptionally powerful magical object. Only an exceptionally powerful confundus charm could have hoodwinked it. Magic way beyond the talents of a fourth year."

Mad-Eye Moody calls the goblet "powerful", and concludes that therefore the wizard overcoming it must be "powerful" as well. We are not told how powerful the goblet is, nor now powerful anything is in relation to the goblet. Harry is protected by "powerful" magic because of Lily's sacrifice, but is that more powerful than the goblet of fire? There is no standard "magic power unit" that can compare the power between things like the goblet or individuals like Dumbledore. The entire system of magic is handwaved with vague adjectives.

(Also, it's so funny that Hermione was like "Dumbledore wouldn't be fooled by stupid workarounds" then Dumbledore is like "did you just get an older student to do it?" Also, the fact that Fred and George were able to cross the age line, but only suffered consequences when the cup spit out the names they put in implies that the goblet detected the deception, not the age line. So what did Dumbledore's age line even do?)

Rowling constructs the Harry Potter world using what Brandon Sanderson would likely call "soft magic". Simplistically, soft magic is usually a system of magic where its limits and mechanisms are not clearly defined.

Beyond analyzing fantasy magic systems, adopting this lens of hard vs soft magic is useful because it can reveal how people intrinsically think about something. One example of this is cybersecurity. There's the "soft magic" version of cybersecurity which is what you see in movies and TV shows. Something about "They breached the mainframe! We have to encrypt the server before they extract our IP address!" But actual cybersecurity is a very technical, mechanistic, and "hard" specialization. Something like SQL injection is a mechanical problem with a mechanical description and can be remedied with an appropriate mechanical solution. It's not about a being a maximally aura-laden hacker to overcome a powerful firewall. Either the attacker finds an effective exploit or they do not, and an exploit is effective if leveraged by anyone, regardless of their "power".

2. Lead and Gold

A few days ago, I had conversation with a friend about the struggle to actually learn things these days. He is a software engineer and studied math in undergrad, so he was especially feeling the impact of AI was having on those communities and fields of study. We both express desire for learning deeply the way we had in the past, before AI.

One way I described that kind of learning is that it felt like the sort of deep understanding that elucidated a "soft" system into a "hard" one. For example, imagine hearing about memoization about the first time. This mysterious paradigm makes your code run super fast by allowing you to sacrifice memory in exchange for time. Externally described, it might as well be a magical incantation or a deal with the devil, transmuting slow, expensive code into a pure function. But, having implemented memoization, I now know how it works, what it costs, and can rationally assess its appropriateness for a given application.

And in this conversation, the thought that came to me is that trying to learn things today feels like trying to do chemistry in the age of alchemy. The thing about alchemy is it actually produced some results. It discovered things like bone porcelain and sulfuric acid. And superficially, alchemy and chemistry can look indistinguishable. It uses a lot of the same equipment. It uses a lot of the same lab techniques. If you put a chemist in an alchemist's workspace, they could probably be able to do quite a bit of chemistry. Plus, alchemy does use real chemistry, and is also somewhat repeatable. An alchemist may use sulfuric acid to etch glass and that will certainly work because it's an actual acid.

But instead of the "hard" substance of understanding molecular bonds, electron orbits, and stoichiometry, alchemy explains the phenomenon it observes with "soft" philosophical ideas. To the alchemist, sulfuric acid is the "oil of vitriol", the essence of fire capable of transforming other substances from their apparent natures. The "soft" process of alchemy can only lead to a "soft" understanding of the process and the outcome. Chemistry is simply a more accurate model for understanding the molecules and atoms of the world and their interactions. The scientific method is simply better a uncovering truth than cryptic alchemical secrecy.

While to a pre-modern outsider, alchemy and chemistry might look functionally indistinguishable. But we, in the enlightened 21st century instinctively feel the difference. Even forgetting the high-stakes stuff like medicine or food safety, I don't know that I'd trust an alchemist to produce a useable bottle of drain cleaner or a useful battery.

But the reason why media can still get away with a "soft" version of cybersecurity is that is that although people who do cybersecruity see it as a "hard" domain, it is generally poorly understood and people are more susceptible to buzz words and hype. And that is where I see similarities with AI.

3. AI as Soft Magic

AI is interesting because like alchemy, your use case determines its nature. A chemist can watching an alchemist use their "oil of vitrol", but be able to understand it from a standpoint of hard science. For the longest time, I obstinately stuck to trying to write as much code as possible by hand. But for a recent project with a close deadline, I tried giving AI a description of a function's params and behavior to let it write the actual code. Doing this usually gave me a small enough chunk of single-responsibility code that I could read over and verify before adding it. For the most part, this worked and made me faster.

But then there's the other use case, and that is when people use AI without examining the output. Note that this is not about knowledge. Plenty of people who know how to code use AI to write code they never read or verify. These cases are often referred to as "vibecoding". While vibecoding can be fun and effective in some situations, I argue that it is always, by its nature, "soft". Claude made this, and Claude is a "powerful wizard", therefore it must not be susceptible to SQL injection, privilege escalation, or use-after-free. Could we verify that by reading through the code base, thereby making the "soft magic" into "hard magic?" Yes we could, but it wrote about 15,000 lines of code, and I leave the office in 30 minutes, so we'll rely on the fact that Claude is a "powerful wizard."

Some may say that this analogy is wrong because vibecoding works and Alchemy doesn't, but this misses the point. Setting aside the fact that Alchemy did genuinely make many discoveries, my argument is not about equating "hard" and "soft" with "it works" and "it doesn't". I use "hard" and "soft" as a heuristic label to represent understanding of an underlying material reality. You may etch glass with "oil of vitriol". I etch it with sulfuric acid. We are both etching glass with the same substance, but your understanding of this process is based in elemental fire as a philosophical category, and mine is based in the reality of ions and titration. We both etch glass, but we are not the same.

Others may say that AI is just the latest layer of abstraction on top of existing abstractions in computing. "Oh, you don't like AI, well, I don't see you writing machine language." And this is also erroneous.

I haven't taken chemistry since high school, but I remember when my classroom would order new supplies. Besides equipment, there would also be raw materials- chemicals we would use in our experiments. Were these things that my teacher may have been able to make herself? Perhaps, but ordering them made her a more effective chemistry teacher since she could use her time for other stuff instead of making quantities of raw materials for us to use. Imagine going to a chemist and saying "you call yourself a chemist? you get so many chemicals off the shelf!" That would be absurd. She was ordering these chemicals from a supplier, but she understood what the chemicals' natures and properties. Even if they would have been difficult or impossible for her to make, she still understood them through the science of chemistry. This is like using an abstraction layer like a compiler or interpreter. You may not have been able to make it yourself, but you understand its behavior and limitations. It is "hard magic" to you.

Conversely, when I was using AI to write func blocks for me, I was giving it a description, then trusting it to make the decisions to fill in the rest with good guesses. A lot of the time it got things right, but sometimes it didn't. The AI was not merely an abstraction because I could not deductively invoke the downstream effects of my prompt the way someone can consistently deductively invoke the downstream effects of code. The same prompt run over and over again leads to different outputs. The same code (with obvious exceptions) will always be the same. When I write a Regex, I don't give it a partial description of what I want, then trust it to make decisions about the rest. I need to define exactly what I want, and if it's wrong, then I got it wrong. That makes my interaction with it one of a "hard magic" paradigm, even if I don't ever think about the implementation behind "import re".

But this insidious drift towards "soft magic" and the adoption of the "but it works" argument is what AI companies are betting on. The promise that the "soft magic" way of doing things is forever going to be as good if not better than the "hard magic" way. Of course you could still do the hard things, and many of us still are. But trying to learn computing in the age of AI feel like trying to be chemists in an age of alchemy. Stoichiometry is hard; why not ruminate on this philosophical principle instead? Programming is hard; why not just vibe it instead? These things are solved problems. Just let us take care of it for you. Buy from us this magical tool that can take your easy alchemy and launder it into hard chemistry for you. We promise it will be just as good.

They want to sell you alchemy so they can own chemistry

They want to sell you astrology so they can own astronomy

They want to sell you claromancy so they can own statistics

4. Dividing Volts by Amps

Clarke's third law is that "any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic." I think Clarke saw this in the progressive sense of encountering advanced civilizations, but now we're seeing it in a regressive one. AI provides a ready excuse for our society to regress from a technological understanding into a magical one.

I started learning coding right pretty much when ChatGPT became publicly available. And at first, it wasn't very good. I would ask it to write pretty simple code and the output would have very basic errors, so I just didn't use it for my first few months of coding. Looking back, I'm glad this was the case. It gave me a great foundation of understanding that I see many others struggle with. Tutorial hell has been replaced by AI hell.

Since then I've developed a nasty habit of picking up interests after AI has "solved" them. For instance, I've picked up drawing. AI images are everywhere, so why draw? Well, precisely because AI has "solved" drawing. In a world where any visual can be instantly generated, my concentrated focus and attention are worth much more. Same with math. So much of math (as a commodity) is solved or solveable with AI. So then that frees me to explore learning the topic in a way centered around exploration, discovery, and wonder.

And I would encourage you to do the same. Paint. Sing. Draw. Write. Code. Learn literary analysis. Statistics. Sound design. Food chemistry. Of course there are moral arguments that AI should not replace humans at these things. But even if we consider the extreme perspective that AI creates a better, cheaper commercialized version of songs, pictures, code, and essays, this just means that you're free from commercialization. Explore, learn, and grow for your own sake.

It's that benefit, the human growth, that I think AI can't imitate. I recently watched an interview with Terence Tao on OpenAI's recent massive proof dump, and he said up until now, proofs were the way that the math community communicated that the person working in an area deeply understood an issue. Now AI can produce proofs en masse, but the point always was, and still will continue to be, to deeply understand an issue.

I think he's right. One thing I'm realizing is that I'd rather live in a world where everyone makes art and no one ever shows anyone else, than a world where no one makes art, but AI art is everywhere.
