--- title: Claude And The Art Of AC Repair subtitle: what using AI to fix my AC taught me about vibe coding created: 2026-08-03 edited: 2026-08-04 tags: ['Lifestyle', 'AI'] ---

One bright Sunday when the raw temp was over 100°F, my family and I discovered, to our horror, that our Air Conditioning had stopped working. We've been in our Central-Texas home for about four years, and although the setup was from the previous homeowner, we've rarely had issues with the AC. I did have to swap the breakers in the panels once in the past, but overall, I'm quite ignorant about AC systems and their maintenance procedures.

WARNING: This is a narrative of and rumination on my experience. Do not do what I did. House currents can kill you. Large capacitors can deliver thousands of volts. Refrigerants are deadly. Compressed pneumatic systems have made many widows. You alone are responsible if you decide to be an idiot.

The first thing I did was open up the breaker panel and toggled the switch. Nothing. Next, I opened the side of the outdoor AC condenser unit to take a look at the dual run capacitor. This is a failure mode that I'd explored the first time the AC had issues, and it seemed like a pretty common failure mode from some research I did then. I pulled the disconnect, discharged the cap, and measured the capacitance with my multimeter. It was reading a little under tolerance. I looked around and nothing else seemed to be obviously wrong, so I made a trip to the hardware store to grab another cap. I also got new fuses while I was at it.

After I got home and installed the new cap and new fuses, the AC was still unresponsive. I looked noticed there was another device inside the AC panel, a black box with wires running out of its exposed contacts. I had no idea what it was, so I pulled the part number and looked it up. Could that be the problem?

While I was sweating inside in front of the computer, I thought to myself "why not ask Claude?" after all, I'm always free to disregard what it says. What could it hurt? But this goes against every instinct. My general approach for generative AI was that it could be helpful, but is unreliable and needs verification. This meant that I only felt comfortable using AI for things that are low-stakes, or are already within or adjacent to a competency I already have, and I was especially wary of situations where I couldn't readily verify the accuracy of the AI's output.

And I did not consider myself as knowledgeable about AC systems at all. I have general electrical knowledge- I solder and tinker around with hardware and know the basics of electromagnetism from AP physics and my own exploration, but never worked on a residential electrical system. But it was a Sunday afternoon and I was unlikely to be able to get someone out to fix it until Monday. Given the ambient temp and the insistence of the hot Texas sun, I decided to give it a shot.

Claude Opus 4.8 (on high) told me to cut the power again and try to spin the motor by hand because the bearings might be shot. I tried it, and the motor spun freely. Then it told me to probe across the motor leads. The voltage across the motor leads was fine. I gave it the part number that I found on the black box, and asked if that could be the issue. It said that what probably happened is that the cap failed and the fan stopped running, but the compressor kept going. This caused the motor windings to cook and open up.

I really feared this outcome, since 1. if this was the case, there's no other option but to purchase a new motor, or even a whole new condenser unit is one can't be found. In desperation, I found that on the side of the black box, there was a button that was popped up. I asked Claude what that was, and it told me it was a thermal overload reset. I asked it what it would do if I pressed it. Claude told me not to, since it would exacerbate the problem by forcing power to the motor in its destroyed state.

I pressed it anyway. And the fan started spinning.

What? Claude told me the motor was cooked. I told Claude this, and it told me that if it's spinning, then it should be able to stay running without re-tripping because it can cool itself as it goes. I pressed the button a few times, but as soon as I let the button go, it would pop back up and everything would stop. I told Claude this, and it said that the button itself might be the issue.

At this point, I started to suspect that Claude didn't actually know what that black box was. I had given it the part number, and it referred to it as "the motor", but it's not strictly the motor, it's a small-ish black box connected to the motor. I told Claude to actually go and do research on the part number I gave it. It went off for like 10 minutes then said that it now realizes the part I'm talking about is actually a contactor, and not a fan motor. Why didn't it do this when I first gave it the part number, or realize along the way that it actually didn't know what that part number was?

With this information, it now told me that the button is actually a plunger to force the contacts closed and is used for technicians to test the unit. So pressing the button was actually good, and tells us that the AC condenser is working. So the good thing now, since the fan spins with the button pressed, is that the AC condenser itself is fine. The problem, however, is that the AC condenser itself is fine, so we're back to square one with any diagnostic.

A few steps ago, Claude had emphasized that I needed to make sure that the thermostat was making an active call for cooling. I thought that this may be issue, especially since I have a stupid "smart" thermostat. I asked Claude about how to get it to actively call for cooling, but Claude told me that there's a cool down period, but if I set the unit to cool, after the period expires there should be an active call. I waited, but nothing happened, and when I tried to reset the thermostat to cool, but it wouldn't turn on at all. I told Claude. It told me to pull the thermostat off the wall and probe the contacts behind it.

I wasn't about to do that, since the thermostat is a single unit and there didn't seem like an easy way to pull it off. I told Claude this, and it told me that if there were power issues to this thermostat unit, there might be an issue with the 24v transformer. I asked it where to find this transformer. It basically told me that it could be anywhere- breaker panel, HVAC closet, by the water heater, in our crawl space, in our attic, or basically any non-bedroom room of the house. I wasn't about to go root around our crawl space or attic, so I was about to throw in the towel, see if we could go over to a friend's for the night, and try to get someone out quickly tomorrow.

But out of desperation, I looked in all the places I could think to check, then decided to look at our furnace blower. I opened it up and found a control board and something that looked like the transformer. I described what I found to Claude, and it told me that I was looking in the right place. It then told me what terminals on the board to probe to see if the 24v transformer was fine. It was. Claude then said that if the board was outputting 24v but the thermostat wasn't getting it, then it's probably a bad terminal connection or a broken wire somewhere in my walls. Hoping it was the easiest thing, I reseated the terminals first, but that didn't fix anything. The one thing left to do was apparently diagnose a break in a wire somewhere in the guts of the house.

To avoid doing that, I started having a look around. I traced one lead from the board through a couple connections to something called a SS2 safety switch. I pulled it out and it sort of looked like a float in a toilet tank. I measured voltage from the board to the switch, and found that the switch was causing the 24v supply to break. I asked Claude what this was, and Claude said that it was a condensate float safety switch that breaks an electrical connection when the float detects water under where the switch is positioned. Claude told me to clear the water in the pan or line, and the switch should allow the 24v to pass unimpeded. This switch was positioned about 8 ft off the ground, so standing on a chair, I got a sponge and started sponging out the water in the pipe opening where the switch sat. I probably did that for 20 minutes, but the water just kept filling back up. There seemed to be an infinite amount of water. I told Claude, and it told me to use a wet/dry vac. Luckily, I had one. If I stood on the chair and held the vacuum in my hand, I could just about get the nozzle into the pipe opening.

After a few minutes with the wet vac, the pipe was finally dry. I reseated the switch, put the panel cover back on, flipped the switch to power it back on, and glory hallelujah, the AC kicked on. At this point, the rest of the family had gone to a neighborhood pool, and the house had gotten up to almost 90°F. I sat inside and watch gleefully as the temps in the house finally started to drop.

This worked for about an hour, but then it died again. Cursing, I went back outside and pulled the SS2 switch out again. There was more water under the switch float, which caused it to kick off again. Same issue as last time. So what was causing the water to accumulate? At this point I was tired of talking to Claude, so I looked up SS2 switches on the internet. It turns out that they're often placed in condensate lines. Because cooling air and equipment causes a lot of condensation, AC setups need a way to drain that out. Often times if the AC is in a basement or below ground, it has to get pumped out. Otherwise, there's just a passive drain line that drips out somewhere. An obstruction or failure of this line means that the condensation backs up and turns the AC off. This is an issue that AC techs regularly address.

I looked around the furnace blower cabinet and saw a pipe dangling out. I figured this was where our drain ought to be, so I attached the wet vac to that instead and ran it. Immediately, the nozzle hose started vibrating as it pulled built-up gunk out of the condensate pipe. The info I found online had videos of people showing you how to pour water in the top of the line in order to help flush out the line entirely. I did this a few times, at least until the gunk chunks stopped coming out. Then reseat the switch, replace the panel, restore the power, and luckily the AC came back on again, and this time it stayed on. By bedtime, the house was under 80°F and the baby slept soundly.

What Does This Have To Do With Vibe Coding?

While I was waiting for the temps in the house to drop back down, I had a chance to sit down and think about how that process went. For one, I was able to fix the AC with the help of AI, which saved us a lot of money and sweat. (We still got a tech out the next day, but since it was just a routine check-up instead of an emergency callout, it was far cheaper, and he verified that I did all the right things.) But while some booster would probably want to point to this and proclaim "AI saves family from death by heat stroke", AI was not uniformly useful for this task.

In fact, there are points where AI was significantly wrong, and it required me doing the opposite of what it said to continue the diagnosis and repair.

* It told me the capacitor was dead and the fan was cooked. They were not.

* It told me not to press the button on the contactor, but I did anyway.

* It told me the contactor was actually a fan motor. It was not.

* It told me the issue was with broken wiring in my walls. I traced leads anyway and found it was not.

At every step of the way, I provided Claude with honest and accurate information, and it still repeatedly mislead me.

I was able to strategically disobey by leaning on a generalized skill set of electrical knowledge- I was able to meter voltage, test continuity, measure resistance in the motor leads, and measure capacitance of the original cap. I also applied a healthy dose of gut intuition on "how things probably work". But with just these skills, I don't think I would have been able to get very far, because need I remind you, I did not really know how AC systems work.

I treated Claude as a conversation partner. I would tell it what I'm seeing and it often responded in three steps:

1. What you're seeing is {X}.

2. That must mean {Y}.

3. You should do {Z}.

Most of the time, it was pretty good with step 1. When I told it I found a SS2 switch, it told me what it did. When I told it about the board I found in the furnace, it told me which terminals should have which voltages. Based on this experience, I would say that AI (at least Claude Opus 4.8) has a lot of generalized knowledge that is very applicable to that first step. In coding, this is most analogous to a looking up information. If I had a question like "What is the syntax for this function?", before AI I would look it up in official docs or a site like Stack Overflow. Now, sometimes I'll ask AI instead, and I've generally found it to be pretty reliable in most experiences.

However, there has been times when I follow the AI's recommendations and the code fails. It turns out that it guessed and when challenged, it will say some version of "let me actually read the docs" before responding. This is similar what happened with the part number for the contactor. It failed to recognize that it needed to look something up because it didn't know it. The difference between these situations where the AI knows enough and where it doens't probably comes down to proportional representation in training data. Common functions, packages, and AC principles are likely well represented but uncommon or specific ideas and mechanisms are not.

Step 2 is where things start to break down. Even if Claude made an accurate assessment, it often would take erroneous leaps of logic. Like when I told it I replaced the capacitor and the fan spins freely, it's conclusion was "the motor windings must be cooked". It took me disobediently pushing the button and telling it to research the contactor part number for it to realize that it was wrong. I see this issue a lot with LLMs. They confidently assert something that's almost always plausible, and sometimes even likely, but always is asserted without consideration of the full context even when it's provided or can be reasonably assumed. It presents a possible conclusion as the dispositive one. Sometimes it's right, but most of the time in this story, it was not. I would say that its performance in step 2 is shaky at best and dubious most of the time.

An AI apologist might at this point say that my criticism of AI's is unfair because how could it know about my specific AC system? This critique is valid, but further highlights the problem. The issue wasn't that it didn't have information, it was that it was drawing conclusions and making assertions as if it did. I would fully respect an AI that was willing to say "it's looking like {X}, but I need {Y} information to confirm", but that is simply not what happened. Claude was blissfully unaware of its lack of awareness.

Step 3 was really a failure. Every single prompt I gave it probably ended in 2-4 things it wanted me to do. If I followed all its commands, the sun probably would have brought the next morning and I would still have power shut off to the house and be trying to find wires my walls. Or I would have given up and sat on my hands and waited for an AC tech or a replacement fan motor to arrive. Or I would have tried to disassemble the motor to probe the windings inside. All of these would have been costly and destructive and provided basically no value. Definitely less than half of its action steps were actually helpful.

This is where the analogy with vibe coding breaks down a little. There are definitely operations in programming that cost resources, but compared to AC repair, trying things in code tends to be far easier. However, the cost is never truly zero, even its just a minimal amount of attention and time. The best safeguard against failures in step 3 is probably to scrutinize step 2. If the AI's reasoning or conclusions seem off, interrogate that before adopting its recommendations.

Making More of the Vibes

From these reflections, we can ask ourselves how can we become better vibe coders (or better vibe AC repairers)? One thing is definitely to pay attention to those three steps. Is it making an observation, a diagnostic conclusion, or a course of action? Identifying these allows users to attach the appropriate level of scrutiny to each one and figure out if what the AI's recommendation is even helpful or accurate. This is a little complicated by the fact that a lot of vibe coding uses the AI to write code directly, so these steps may be hidden from the user. But you can always explicitly ask for them. Something like "You wrote this API interface as stateless. Why?" can help elucidate the black box of steps within the AI.

Additionally, a tremendous advantage came from having some preexisting knowledge (in my case, basic electrical knowledge) and insubordinate skepticism. Applied to vibe coding, this probably looks like becoming better at actual coding. I see this like an experienced team member who gets placed in a supervisory/managerial role. Although they are no longer actively doing the building, they can supervise effectively because they are knowledge about the underlying domain from internalized expertise.

The opposite of this is letting the AI lead you down into every rabbit hole and idea it gets excited enough to suggest. That's how you end up thousands of lines of code into a project and finding out that there was a basic incompatibility or fundamental system design issue. As much as AI can accelerate writing good code, it can also accelerate writing bad code.

Happy Medium

This experience showed me two possible extremes. Without AI at all, I probably would have been able to pull all the part numbers, find their technical documentation, and looked up the systems and concepts along the way, but it would have taken way longer. If I fully followed AI, I would probably have given up or destroyed my house. Similarly, a lot of people fearmonger that AI is making everyone stupid and decaying their critical reasoning and subject literacy. In my case, I actually understand AC systems better through this experience of using AI. On the other hand, a lot of people say that AI is going to lead us to whatever sci-fi utopia they find most persuasive. In my case, it took active defiance of the AI to be able to find the issue and fix it.

I found that I ended up in a place that was similar to a view articulated by Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor in their paper and writings on AI as Normal Technology:

We articulate a vision of artificial intelligence (AI) as normal technology. To view AI as normal is not to understate its impact—even transformative, general-purpose technologies such as electricity and the internet are “normal” in our conception. But it is in contrast to both utopian and dystopian visions of the future . . .

Although I disagree with them on certain issues such as the necessity of changes in public policy, their "normal" approach is often a refreshing change from the extreme skeptic to extreme booster dichotomy. This experience showed me that "middle path" first hand. In the end, I was able to fix my AC. If it goes out next time, I can take the same approach and because I know much more than I knew before, probably diagnose and repair the issue much more effectively. Then the time after that, even moreso. That realization is a strange one, because it has a sort of human optimism. I have this tool that I can use to grow my core abilities over time. This sort of perspective rarely exists in AI discourse generally, especially at the two extremes of the spectrum.

But that doesn't happen automatically. Especially with coding, where the AI can write, test, and run all your code for you, it takes active effort to learn and grow. It's so easy to just pop in a prompt and roll with whatever the AI outputs. This is as true of coding as anything else. The decision to use AI as a tool for your development and not simply outsource your decision making, intuition, imagination, or critical thinking is a challenging one. In this case, my motivation was to fix my AC and not destroy my house, but it showed me what it looks like to make that choice and find that middle growth path. In the future, even if I'm not faced with these types of consequences, I hope I can identify what that choice looks like and have the courage to make it.
