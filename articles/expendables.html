I will admit it. I liked the Expendables franchise. Was it good writing? Meh. Good action? Meh. Good acting? Meh. It enjoyed it the same way someone enjoys a frozen burger from the freezer section of a democratized grocery store, thrown on a grill in the back yard. Nothing special, but a simple pleasure for what it is.

The first expendables was good. One thing I really enjoyed was the fact that you actually got to know the personalities of the crew. Everyone on the crew, even those without major speaking roles or plot plots, were given enough of a personality to be a person. The story was contrived, of course, with a predictable plot and eye-roll moments, but all in all, quite an enjoyable action flick.

The second one was pretty quality as well. After the success of the first, It seemed like a lot of the rest of the classic action cast from Stallone's era really wanted to have their piece of the action too, so we see people like Schwartzeneger's "I'll be back"s and Willis' "yippee ki yay"s showing up, plus Jean-Claude Van Damme and Chuck Norris. It seemed sort of like a nod to that era of action hero film, and although it took away some of the tighter and more focused writing that One had, it still wasn't all that bad.

Three was a pretty significant departure from One and Two, but by design. The whole point is that the old crew was getting old, so to act in their best interest and try to find a younger crew. But, predictably, the old crew is upset about getting cut out, denies they're getting older, and decides to barge in the young crew's mission, showing up at just the right time for everyone to solve the world's problems together in the most violent and spectacular way humanly possible. The new additions weren't super individualized, but they had stories and identities, even if those were largely mapped onto the roles from the old crew. But that was the point, and made the replacement feel that much more personal.

Why the Expendables?

When I first saw the trailer for Expendables 4, or "Expend4bles" I guess, I was excited. After all, I'd had a good time watching the rest of the franchise so far, and I saw that some new characters were in it. Tony Jaa is hands down, one of the best on-screen martial artists alive, and I was super excited to see what he could do. I had liked Jet Li when he showed up in the first expendables, but felt like his character was played off as just the Napoleon complex little guy character to a certain extent. I wanted to see if Tony Jaa would be given the writing and screen time to really show the craziness and mastery that he's famous for.

So when I started 4, I was initially quite excited, and the first thing that made me do a double take was the visual language. 1 and 2 were gritty. Like dark, occluded scenes where only catch light in someone's eye from the moon or a fire is visible. I loved that aesthetic, and it really set a great tone for the franchise. 3 leaned more towards big, clean spectacle, but wasn't as much of a departure. 4, however, looks like it got straight up yassified. Megan Fox (yes, Megan Fox, has exactly zero pores on her face and at every point of the plot, has lipstick like she just came off a runway. Compare this with how Yu Nan is portrayed in 2 or even Ronda Rousey in 3, and this was clearly a deliberate choice.

Then there was a heavy instagram-style color filer that really leaned into the whole blue/orange thing in a lot scenes. While I don't necessarily hate this visual design, I feel like it just doesn't really fit with the theme of the franchise.

So what about the cast? Due to different commitments and priorities, Expendables have always had a bit of a rotating cast. This movie is a no different, with a few new faces around the old crew. Barney, Christmas, Gunner, and Toll Road were all there. To this, we added Galan (Jacob Scipio), Gina (Megan Fox), Easy Day (50 Cent), and Lash (Levy Tran). Gina is Christmas' love interest, and the movie starts with them in a relationship. That's fine I guess, but we don't really get any back story with being an expendable or how she knows Christmas and the other expendables. Galan is Galgo's son, who was an expendable played previously by Antonio Banderas, so that sort of makes sense, and he has a similar vibe of being way too energetic, uncomfortably raunchy, and overly verbose. Easy Day is explained as a former special forces operator who had worked with some other members of the team. Okay, ham-fisted, but fine I guess.

But Lash? Who is lash? No idea. She just kind shows up in the middle of the movie, and the thing that makes her stand out is that she 1. uses a whip/chain/lash. 2. Nothing else, really.

And while the older Expendable movies used action, writing, and fight choreography to develop the characters, for a large part, every role in 4 is more or less or interchangeable. For instance, compare these two dynamics between the first Expendables and the 4th: Toll Road, played by Randy Couture, a real-life former professional fighting champion, has cauliflower ears. He's both proud of them and sensitive about them. In the first movie, someone makes an offhanded remark about his appearance generally, and Toll Road gets set off on a spiel about how he wrestled in college, and that one of the side effects of blunt force "trauma to the ear" is cauliflower ears. When he get to this line, the camera pans over to Caesar, who mouths along, indicating that they've all heard this unsolicited soap box before. The other men in the circle also roll their eyes, and Christmas puts his head in his hands. Clearly, this is a side of Toll Road they know all too well.

Later, in one of the parts of a multi-stage final fight, the expendables are faced with Paine, the henchman of the main protagonist played by Stone Cold Steve Austin. They've faced him before, and it has not gone well for them. While Barney is trying to kill the main protagonist and rescue the girl, they run through this ring of fire type thing, and Paine is right on their tail. Suddenly, Toll Road comes soaring out of the darkness and engages Paine. While there has been martial fight elements to pretty much every fight, The majority of it is striking or flashy throws. This scene, however, showcases how Toll Road's move set is still heavy on wrestling. He ends by suplexing Paine into the flames, where he gets totally Anakin Skywalkered.

The setup is that Toll Road is both proud and sensitive about his cauliflower ears, and then the payoff is that he gets to use his wrestling to take down an important antagonist. Toll Road is vindicated in the value of his experience and his past by how he can provide unique value to the team.

Contrast this with the cauliflower ears in the 4th movie. When new characters are introduced, he twice has the chance to explain his cauliflower ears to them. Once to Easy Day, and again to Lash. You sort of expect, then, that his wrestling, symbolized by his ears, will have an important role in the fight. Do they? Nope. He gets shanked pretty early by a knife, and slowly bleeds out for the majority of the final fight, creating a ticking clock for them to finish up and get him medical help.

So that's the ticking clock right? The Expendables have to decide to save Toll Road or finish the mission? Nope. There's a literal nuclear bomb with a countdown timer to put urgency on the plot.

On so like the 3rd movie, stopping the bomb from going off is the whole mission? Nope, they're stopping a guy name Ocelot, and the bomb goes off anyway once Barney sinks it under the sea, so all this time they spent looking for the kill switch was basically useless.

Oh so like with Jean-Claude Van Damme or Mel Gibson, there's a final fight with the villain that pushes One of the Expendables to the edge of their physical, mental, and physical strength? Nope, Barney swoops in on a helicopter and kills Ocelot with the guns.

Oh so Barney's leadership of the crew lead got them to a place where, although unexpected, the crew is able to complete the mission and save the world? Nope, for most of the movie, Barney is presumed dead and is entirely absent.

You're probably seeing a pattern by now. Again and again, this movie has the chance to do something right, to hand Chekov a gun and tell him to fire it at a bad guy, and they simply don't.

Chekov's Amnesia

The most frustrating thing is that there were so many chances where they could have made the movie so much more than it was. When Lash first gets introduced, she and Toll Road have a little bit of a flirty exchange. In another thing, Lash tries to take a cheeky peak while Toll Road relieves himself. That's the set up and the reminder. The payoff would have been if Lash somehow saved Toll Road, helped him in a unique way, or even provided companionship in a non-generic way when Toll Road get injured. Maybe in the fight they gang up and synergies so well because they're on the same wavelength. Maybe Toll Road runs out of ammo and Lash comes in clutch. There are so many ways that this set up and reminder could have paid off.

But none of that happens. Toll Road gets stabbed, and Lash just kinda holds him, but like Easy Day is just as close. Then in the final bar scene, after the baddies have been vanquished, Lash and Toll Road are again just a little flirty. That's it. Set up, reminder, and whiff.

The same with another story that could have been told. Gunner's drinking shows up a few times in the film. He's first mentioned as being sober, but he's also wearing glasses and from his sniping, the movie shows us that he's trigger shy and can't see so well, leading to him missing some shots. When the action gets heavy, he gets to a point to where he says to hell with it, pulls out a flask, takes a long swig, and kills some bad guys. Okay so there's the setup and the reminder. Gunner was trying to change his life so he quit drinking, but now finds that he needs it to maintain his professional performance. A satisfying payoff might be something like Gunner running out of booze and realizing he actually doesn't need it after all, or drinking so much he learns that there's a limit to how much alcohol helps him. But none of that happens. Set up, reminder, whiff.

The last big thing I think they could have done something with is Galan's talking. His father, Galgo, basically talked the ear off of anyone within a ten foot range of him in Expendables 3. Galan, as his son, shows the same trait, and everyone likewise hates him. But after Barney's alleged death, he suddenly turns super quiet. Was Barney like a father to him? Were they super close? No, in fact in Expendables 3, we find out that Barney has avoided Galgo like the plague for as long as he could. How are Galan connected to Barney in a way that the others aren't? It's never really explained. Plus, Galan never gets his voice back in any meaningful way to close out this arc. He was just chatty, then he wasn't, then maybe he was again the final bar scene? Who knows.

The most disappointing thing was how Tony Jaa... wasn't a character. He was literally a character, but not really a person with any depth. He briefly gives a story about how he knew Barney, but gave up a life of violence because he didn't agree with what it was making him. Then in a later action scene, he's decides to be violent again, since, you know, it's an action movie. But all the reasons he provides are weak and superficial, vaguely disguised as appeals to the tenants of an undefined religion the audience is likely to find exotic and unfamiliar. All this would be forgivable if we got fight scenes that really showcased what Jaa could do, but we don't. He is fight choreo is, stylistically, is pretty disappointing. Not only is it inherently unremarkable when compared to what Jaa is capable of, the camera work and photography gives him the pretty uniform fast-cut swing-cam treatment.

Just for a sanity check, I went back and watched the Jet Li fight with Dolph Lundgren in Expendables I, and oh my goodness it was actually so much better. Gunner is a brash bully, swinging heavy, fast haymakers with the weight of his arms and body. Jet Li is tactical and fast, but knows he isn't match. His frustration is visible in how Gunner forces him to tank blows instead of dodging them, strategizing how to use the environment to his advantage. While it's also quite fast-action swing-cam, the choreography and photography at least depict a sequence of events that is more than action for action's sake, which is how a lot of Expendables 4 felt.

It really felt like the movie I was watching was actually cut down for TV and left out critical elements of character and story development.

Why?

All this reminds of Dan Olsen's review of Suicide Squad. He explains how the audience knows that Katana, like Lash, is basically a throwaway character because of the way the character is introduced and handled. He also explains many instances of setup, reminder, then whiff.

And the question I'm left with is why? No one's expecting Hamlet up here. They could make a lot of compromises, and as long as it's a fun action movie staring everyone's favorite leads from past franchises, it would surely be successful.

But maybe that mentality is wrong. The first film was actually directed by Stallone, and I think it really shows. It has a lot of that Rocky grit and heart. By movie 2, a chunk of the cast had rotated out, but a lot of big names like Chuck Norris and Jean-Claude Van Damme were brought on. It still really felt like a celebration of these high-profile action heroes. By movie 3, I think a lot of the cast saw the direction the wind was blowing. It stopped being about the craft of an action movie and started to become just a cash grab, the same ways Avengers or Star Wars eventually devolved. By movie 4, actually only four of the original expendables came back. And considering that Barney was presumed dead for most of the movie and therefore absent, arguably three of the original expendables showed up.

In fact, churn through the franchise was seemingly always the norm. Directors, producers, editors are all a revolving door. Even the composer quit after 3. A lot of this could be attributed to the fast that the first movie was made in 2010, the second in 2012, the third in 2014, then the fourth in 2023. In that 9 year gap, many people probably just moved on to other projects, but I think that if 4 was promising, at least some of them would have come back.

The drop in quality was also reflected unambiguously in the box office performance. With a budget of anywhere from $80-100 million, past films always doubled if not tripled their budget in the box office. However, Expendables 4's $100 million budget bagged them $51 million worldwide.

Expendables 5

So while I was looking all this info up, one site I found particularly useful was the Wikipedia page on the franchise, and I noticed something odd. Expendables 4 was not the last movie in the franchise. A movie with a TBD release date was listed under it. It was called "Expendabelles" and yes, according to press coverage, it is exactly what you think it is. An all-female addition to the franchise. And at that, I shudder. Not because I'm against women staring in action movies, in fact, quite the opposite. Rather, because I think that women in action films has so much untapped potential that unfortunately rarely seen. And looking at Expendables 4, it really looks like we're stuck in the hell of design by committee whose priorities are to always only make the safest move and deliver a film that is objectionable to no one, not caring if it actually appeals to anyone.

And while the cynical part of my brain isn't always right, it is, often times, entertaining. While I was putting more thought into this movie than the writers did and ruminating on how the characters and story are handled, I couldn't help but think that these production companies probably see people in the real world the same way they handle these characters. That is to say, narrative media allows those who consume it to expose themselves to the lives and stories of people who are not them. As George R. R. Martin famously said, "But I have lived a thousand lives. I have loved a thousand loves. I’ve wandered distant worlds and seen the end of time because I read." But to be open to this inherently requires a degree of sympathy and humanity. You have to be open to the experience of that other life for you to live, love, wander, or see as that character does.

Increasingly, it feels like our society is moving away from that fundamental idea of how people relate to each other, and it shows up in our media.
